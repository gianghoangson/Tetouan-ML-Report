\section{Dataset and Exploratory Data Analysis}\label{sec:data}
\subsection{Data and quality checks}
The dataset\cite{uci} contains \num{52416} ten-minute records from 1 January 2017 00:00 to 30 December 2017 23:50 (364 days): temperature, humidity, wind speed, two diffuse-flow (solar radiation) measurements and the loads of three distribution zones (Quads, Smir, Boussafou). Averaging each hour gives \num{8736} hourly rows. The target is the hourly mean of the three-zone sum, in kW (\cref{tab:descriptive}).

Data-quality checks find no missing values, duplicates, irregular time steps or non-positive values, so no row is removed or imputed. Two anomalies are kept as they are. On 20 April at noon, load moves from Zone 1 to Zone 2 for a few hours; the city total barely changes, which is one reason to forecast the total. Wind speed is almost binary (57\% of samples $\le 0.1$, 38.6\% between 4.8 and 5.1), which suggests an on/off instrument signal; it is kept and listed as a limitation. Unless stated otherwise, the figures below use January--November only, so that the December test set does not influence feature design.

\subsection{Seasonal pattern}
\Cref{fig:annual} shows a flat load of about \num{66000}--\num{70000} kW from January to May, a summer rise to a July peak of \num{88174} kW (about 30\% above the base level), and a quick fall in September. December, the test month, averages \num{63751} kW, close to November (\num{65105} kW). The summer peak matters later: models trained on the first half of the year must forecast July loads higher than anything seen in training.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{F01_total_hourly_year.png}
\caption{Hourly total load over 2017 with a centred seven-day mean (F01). December (test) is shaded.}\label{fig:annual}
\end{figure}

The three zones behave differently (\cref{fig:zones}). Zone 3 rises most in summer (its July mean is 2.6 times its December mean), and from September Zone 2 grows while Zone 3 falls, consistent with load transfers between networks. Forecasting the city total avoids these transfers.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{F02_zones_daily.png}
\caption{Daily mean load of the three distribution zones (F02).}\label{fig:zones}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Daily and weekly cycles}
Every season has the same daily shape (\cref{fig:season}): a minimum at 06:00--07:00, a daytime plateau and an evening peak at 19:00--20:00, about twice the minimum. The daily cycle is the strongest structure in the data and motivates the hour features and the daily lags. Summer is higher at every hour, most visibly at night.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.9\linewidth]{F03_profile_by_season.png}
\caption{Mean daily load profile by season, January--November (F03).}\label{fig:season}
\end{figure}

The right panel of \cref{fig:weekday} removes the daily cycle by comparing each hour with the Monday--Friday mean of the same hour. Sundays are 5.9\% lower on average and about 15\% lower at 07:00--08:00; Saturday mornings are 4--6\% lower. A ``copy yesterday'' forecast will therefore be too low on Mondays and too high on Sundays, which the baseline results confirm (\cref{sec:baseline}).
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{F04_heatmap_hour_weekday.png}
\caption{Mean load by hour and weekday (left) and deviation from the Monday--Friday mean of the same hour (right), January--November (F04).}\label{fig:weekday}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Ramadan and holidays}
Ramadan (27 May -- 25 June) reshapes the daily profile (\cref{fig:ramadan}): the load between 00:00 and 03:00 is up to \num{32142} kW higher than in the four preceding weeks (the pre-dawn meal), mornings are lower and evenings higher. Mean temperature is only \SI{1.8}{\celsius} higher, so weather does not explain the change; this motivates an \code{is_ramadan} flag.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.93\linewidth]{F05_ramadan_profile.png}
\caption{Hourly load profile during Ramadan and the four preceding weeks, holidays excluded (F05).}\label{fig:ramadan}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:holidays} compares each day with the median of the surrounding 15 days, which removes the seasonal trend. Sundays are 5.9\% lower. Religious holidays drop sharply (Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha: $-8$\% to $-13$\%, mean about $-9$\%), whereas civil holidays are weaker and uneven (mean about $-3$\%; 1 May and 6 November about $-6$\%, other dates close to normal days). The holiday flag is therefore split into religious and civil holidays.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{F08_weekday_holiday.png}
\caption{Daily load by weekday and on holidays, as deviation from the centred 15-day median, January--November (F08).}\label{fig:holidays}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Weather and autocorrelation}
Temperature is the weather variable most correlated with the load ($r=0.49$; humidity $-0.30$, wind speed 0.21, radiation 0.15 and 0.02). The relation is nonlinear (\cref{fig:temperature}): the load barely depends on temperature below about \SI{22}{\celsius} and rises quickly above it (cooling). The three time-of-day bands show that the hour explains more than temperature, which favours nonlinear models. The zones are strongly correlated with the total (\cref{fig:correlation}) but cannot be inputs at the same timestamp, since they are components of the target.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.8\linewidth]{F06a_temperature_vs_total.png}
\caption{Temperature against total load, coloured by time of day, January--November (F06a).}\label{fig:temperature}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.75\linewidth]{F06b_correlation.png}
\caption{Pearson correlations between hourly variables, January--November (F06b).}\label{fig:correlation}
\end{figure}

The autocorrelation function (\cref{fig:acf}) peaks every 24 hours: 0.98 at lag 24, slowly decaying to 0.95 at lag 72; it is negative ($-0.23$) at lag 12, when day meets night. The value at lag 168 (0.95) stands above its neighbours (0.88 at lags 167 and 169), revealing a weekly cycle. These peaks justify \code{lag_24}, \code{lag_48} and the tested \code{lag_168} for \hday, and \code{lag_1}--\code{lag_3} for \hone. They also mean that Naive-24h is a hard baseline to beat.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{F07_acf_total.png}
\caption{Autocorrelation of the hourly total load up to lag 200, January--November (F07).}\label{fig:acf}
\end{figure}
