\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Electricity distributors plan purchases and dispatch one day ahead, so they need a forecast of every hour of the next day. This project forecasts the hourly electricity load of Tetouan City (Morocco), using the UCI Power Consumption of Tetouan City dataset\cite{uci}. The target is the city total, the sum of the three distribution-zone loads, averaged per hour.

\textbf{Main problem (\hday, day-ahead).} At 00:00 of day $D$ the model forecasts the 24 hourly loads of day $D$. The most recent known load is that of 23:00 on day $D-1$, so every load-history feature must lag the target by at least 24 hours.

\textbf{Secondary problem (\hone).} The same target forecast one hour ahead. It is used only to show how much the forecast horizon changes the difficulty.

The load has a very strong daily cycle, so ``tomorrow equals today'' is already a strong forecast. A model is useful only if it beats such a seasonal naive baseline. Moreover, ten-minute records are strongly correlated: a randomly shuffled split lets a model interpolate between neighbouring points and report very high accuracy without forecasting anything. The project therefore uses a strictly chronological evaluation. December is reserved as the test set and used once, after all choices have been fixed on January--November.

The study answers three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] How much do conventional ML models (linear regression, decision tree, random forest, SVR and XGBoost) improve on seasonal naive baselines under chronological evaluation?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] How much does each feature group (weather, calendar, special days, load history) contribute?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] How do hyperparameter tuning and the target formulation affect accuracy and computational cost?
\end{enumerate}
Two supplementary experiments compare the \hday\ and \hone\ horizons, and measure how common evaluation shortcuts (shuffled splits, very short horizons) inflate reported accuracy.

The contributions are: (i) a chronological pipeline with baselines, five expanding-window folds and a single December test; (ii) a lag-relative target that removes the tree models' failure to extrapolate during the July heat wave; (iii) a feature ablation; and (iv) a leakage experiment that explains why accuracies reported online are higher but not comparable.
